The invisible leaks

Every lost customer

left through a gap you didn't see.

01

Response time

An inquiry answered in three days is often already lost. Speed signals interest and competence. Many businesses lose customers and applicants purely to a slow reply — while blaming price or the market.

02

The first impression

The website, the first call, the first email. If it's confusing, generic or unprofessional, the prospect quietly leaves — and you never learn why. First impressions are cheap to fix and expensive to ignore.

03

No follow-through

The inquiry that got one reply and then silence. The applicant who never heard back. Most losses happen not from rejection but from a follow-up that simply never happened.

04

Unclear next step

If the customer or applicant doesn't know what happens next, they stall. A clear, simple next step at every stage keeps momentum — its absence loses people who were ready to proceed.

FAQ

Why do customers leave without complaining?

Most dissatisfied customers never complain — they just quietly leave and don't return. This makes the loss invisible: you don't get feedback, you get silence. The causes are usually mundane — a slow response, a small friction, an unclear process. Because they never complain, these leaks persist unless you deliberately look for them.

Why do good applicants drop out of the process?

The same reasons customers leave: slow response, unclear process, no follow-through. Good applicants have options and read a slow or confusing process as disinterest or disorganization. An applicant who waits two weeks for a reply has usually accepted another offer — and you never learn you lost them to your own process, not to a competitor's salary.

How do I find out why I'm losing customers?

Look at the journey from the customer's side, stage by stage: how fast is the first response, how clear is the first impression, what happens after the first contact, is the next step always obvious? Often a single walk-through reveals leaks you'd stopped noticing. Exit feedback from lost customers and applicants, where obtainable, is gold.

Is it cheaper to keep customers than win them?

Almost always. Winning a new customer typically costs several times more than keeping an existing one. Yet most businesses spend heavily on acquisition and little on retention. Closing the leaks that lose existing customers is usually the highest-return, lowest-cost lever available — and the most overlooked.
