The causes

Liquidity problems

01

Payment terms

You pay suppliers in 14 days, customers pay you in 60. That gap is financed from your own pocket — often unnoticed until it hurts.

02

No planning

Without a liquidity forecast, every bottleneck is a surprise. A 13-week rolling forecast turns surprises into decisions.

03

The growth trap

Growth binds capital: more inventory, more staff, more pre-financing. Fast growth without liquidity planning is a classic insolvency cause.

One customer at 40% of revenue is a liquidity risk, not a success. If they pay late — or leave — the whole structure wobbles.

FAQ

What is a 13-week liquidity forecast?

A rolling forecast of all cash-in and cash-out over the next 13 weeks, updated weekly. Thirteen weeks is roughly a quarter — long enough to see problems coming, short enough to stay precise. It's the standard tool in restructuring and one of the highest-leverage steering instruments for any SME.

When is liquidity critical?

When the forecast shows a gap you can't close from operations. Warning signs: consistently stretching supplier payments, using the tax office as a credit line, revenue growing but the account not, and having no idea what next month looks like. The best time to act is before it gets tight.

What's the difference between profit and liquidity?

Profit is an accounting result over a period. Liquidity is whether there's money in the account today to pay what's due. A business can be profitable and still insolvent — because profit doesn't say when money arrives. Liquidity is about timing, not totals.

How quickly can liquidity be stabilized?

First measures often show effect within weeks: renegotiated terms, accelerated receivables, timed expenses. Building a robust liquidity system with forecast and steering takes 30–90 days. In an acute crisis, a rapid stabilization plan comes first.
