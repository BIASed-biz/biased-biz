Strategy Examples · Practice · Positioning · SME

Strategy sounds abstract.

Until you see it in your own industry.

'Positioning', 'differentiation', 'competitive advantage' — strategy vocabulary stays abstract until it's made concrete in a real business. So here are three examples across different industries, showing what a clear strategy actually looks like versus its absence.

These aren't templates to copy — every business is different. They show the pattern of strategic thinking: the choices that turn a generic business into a positioned one.

Example 1

The carpenter

who stopped doing everything.

A carpentry workshop did everything: kitchens, staircases, repairs, shopfitting, the occasional coffin. Fully booked, working hard, thin margins, constant stress. The owner worked 60-hour weeks and wondered why little was left over.

The strategic shift: focus on high-end fitted staircases for architects. One clear segment, premium pricing, a reputation that spread among a specific customer group. Revenue fell in the first year. Profit rose. Stress dropped. The deliberate 'no' to everything else was the entire strategy.

Example 2

The IT service

that picked one industry.

An IT services firm served anyone with a computer problem — retail, manufacturing, medical practices, law firms. Broad market, interchangeable offer, price pressure against every other generalist in the region.

The strategic choice: specialize in IT for medical practices. Understanding their specific software, compliance and workflows. Suddenly not a generalist competing on price but the specialist practices recommend to each other. Same skills, radically different position — and pricing power that followed.

Example 3

The tax advisor

who stopped competing on hours.

A tax advisory practice billed by the hour like everyone else — and competed with every other practice on price and availability. Good work, invisible in a crowded market, always under time pressure.

The strategic reposition: become the specialist for a specific client type — say, e-commerce businesses with their particular VAT, inventory and cross-border questions. Fixed packages instead of hourly billing. A clear reason to choose them. The expertise was always there; the strategy made it visible and chargeable.

FAQ

What makes a good business strategy?

A good strategy makes clear choices: a specific target customer, a distinct way of winning, and a deliberate list of what to leave out. It's specific enough that it rules things out. 'We offer quality and service' is not a strategy — every competitor says the same. 'We are the specialist for X, serving Y, and we don't do Z' is.

How do I find the right strategy for my business?

Start with the Pareto question: which 20% of your customers and products create 80% of your profit? That usually points toward your natural strength. A clear strategy often means doing more of what already works best — and deliberately dropping the rest that drains energy for little return.

Can a small business even have a strategy?

Especially a small business needs one. A corporation can afford to be spread across many segments; an SME can't. The smaller the company, the more important focus becomes. The examples above are all small businesses — and the strategy was in each case a sharper focus, not a bigger ambition.

How is strategy different for SMEs than for corporations?

Corporations manage portfolios across many markets. SMEs win by focus — being the clear best choice for a specific customer in a specific niche. For an SME, strategy is less about complex portfolio decisions and more about the courage to specialize and say no to everything outside the chosen focus.
