01

Positioning before product

Who exactly is this for, and why would they choose it? Founders in love with their product often skip this — and build something nobody specifically wants. Positioning comes first.

02

Test the model cheaply

Before investing heavily, test whether people actually pay. The cheapest validation is a real customer with real money. Assumptions are expensive; evidence is cheap.

03

Get the structure right

Legal form, co-founder agreements, equity splits, roles. Boring to think about, catastrophic to get wrong. These are cheap to set up correctly and painful to unwind.

04

Plan the money honestly

How long until profitability, how much runway, what's the real burn? Optimistic founders underestimate this consistently. Honest numbers at the start prevent a crisis later.

FAQ

What are the most common startup mistakes?

The most expensive ones happen early and quietly: building a product before confirming anyone wants it, no clear positioning, unclear co-founder agreements, the wrong legal structure, and optimistic financial planning. Most startup failures trace back not to the market but to foundation decisions made too fast at the start.

How do I know if my business idea works?

Test it as cheaply as possible before investing heavily. The strongest validation is a real customer paying real money — not friends saying they like it. Talk to potential customers, make a small offer, see if anyone buys. Evidence beats conviction. If nobody will pay a little now, more product won't change that.

Which legal form should I choose?

It depends on liability, tax, number of founders and growth plans — and it's worth getting right early because changing later is costly. This is a decision to make with proper advice (tax advisor, lawyer) rather than by default. BI³AS³ed helps you understand the strategic implications; the formal setup belongs with specialists.

Do I need a business plan?

You need clarity more than a document. A 40-page business plan for a drawer is wasted effort. But the thinking behind it — who the customer is, how you win, what it costs, how long the money lasts — is essential. Capture it in a way you'll actually use. Clarity is the goal; the document is just a means.
