What strategy really is

Strategy is not a plan.

It's a set of choices.

Many owners confuse strategy with planning. A plan says what you'll do. A strategy says why — and, more importantly, what you won't do. The hardest part of strategy is the deliberate 'no': the customers you don't serve, the products you don't build, the markets you don't enter.

A clear strategy makes daily decisions easier because it gives a yardstick. Without one, every opportunity looks worth taking — and the business spreads itself thin across all of them.

01

Where do you play?

Which markets, which customers, which segments? Not 'everyone' — that's the absence of a choice. The sharper the definition, the stronger the position.

02

How do you win?

What makes a customer choose you over the alternative? If the answer is 'we're a bit cheaper' or 'we try harder', there is no real strategy yet.

03

What do you leave out?

The deliberate no. Every strong strategy sacrifices something. A business that wants to be everything to everyone has decided nothing.

04

Does the team know it?

A strategy only the owner knows isn't a strategy — it's a secret. It has to be simple enough to guide the decisions of everyone in the company.

FAQ

What's the difference between strategy and planning?

Planning defines what you'll do and when. Strategy defines why and what you deliberately won't do. Planning is execution; strategy is choice. Most SMEs plan well but have no explicit strategy — which is why they struggle to say no and end up spread thin across too many things.

How long does a strategy process take?

A focused strategy sprint for an SME takes a few weeks, not months. The goal isn't a 100-page document but a clear, one-page strategy the team understands. BI³AS³ed works fast and pragmatic — enough analysis to decide well, not analysis for its own sake.

Why do so many SME strategies fail?

Because they stay abstract, aren't communicated, or don't guide real decisions. A strategy that lives in a document but not in daily choices is decoration. The test of a strategy is whether it changes what people say no to.

Do we really need a written strategy?

You need a clear, shared one — written helps because it forces precision and lets you communicate it. The value isn't the document; it's the clarity it creates. If everyone in leadership can state the same three answers — where you play, how you win, what you leave out — you have a strategy.
