The costly biases

They're not stupidity.

They're how every mind works.

01

Confirmation bias

Seeing the evidence that supports what you already believe, ignoring the rest. It's why owners persist with a strategy long after the market said no — they only noticed the confirming signals.

02

Sunk cost fallacy

Continuing a failing project because of what's already been invested. 'We've put too much in to stop now' is the sentence that turns a manageable loss into a catastrophic one.

03

Overconfidence

Systematically overrating your own judgment, estimates and control. It's why projects run over, risks get underweighted, and 'it won't happen to us' precedes so many crises.

04

Anchoring

The first number sets the frame for everything after. In negotiation this is a weapon — whoever anchors first shapes the range. Understanding it protects you and, used deliberately, serves you.

FAQ

What are cognitive biases?

Cognitive biases are systematic, predictable errors in human judgment — not random mistakes but consistent patterns in how every mind processes information and decides. Examples include confirmation bias (favoring evidence that supports existing beliefs), sunk cost fallacy (continuing because of past investment), and overconfidence. They're universal wiring, not personal weakness — which is why even smart, experienced people fall for them without deliberate awareness.

How do biases affect business decisions?

Constantly and expensively. An owner in the grip of confirmation bias persists with a failing strategy because they only notice confirming signals. Sunk cost keeps unprofitable projects alive. Overconfidence causes underestimated risks and overrun projects. Because biases feel like clear thinking from the inside, they're invisible without deliberate checks — which is exactly what makes them costly for decision-makers who don't know them.

Can biases be used as a tool?

Yes — the same biases that trap you can serve you when understood. Anchoring shapes negotiations: the first number frames the range, so setting it deliberately is an advantage. Framing shapes how offers are received. Understanding these isn't manipulation for its own sake — it's competence. Knowing how minds actually judge lets you communicate and negotiate far more effectively, and defend against those using the same levers on you.

How do I make better decisions despite my biases?

You can't eliminate biases, but you can build checks against them: deliberately seek evidence against your preferred view (counters confirmation bias), ask 'would I start this today?' about ongoing projects (counters sunk cost), and get an outside perspective from someone willing to disagree. A sparring partner who challenges your thinking is one of the most effective bias-defenses an owner can have.
