The well-meant traps

The habits that feel like virtue

are often the ceiling.

01

Working in the business, not on it

The owner who's the best technician, salesperson and problem-solver becomes the bottleneck for all three. Time spent in the work is time not spent building the business that could do the work without them.

02

Not delegating

'It's faster if I do it myself' is true once and false forever after. Every task the owner keeps is a task the business can't scale. Delegation feels like losing control; it's actually how you gain capacity.

03

No focus — saying yes to everything

Every opportunity taken is focus lost. The owner who serves every customer, offers every service, chases every lead has decided nothing — and spreads the business too thin to excel at anything.

04

Confusing busy with productive

A full day feels productive. But motion isn't progress. The hardest owner discipline is stepping back from the busywork to ask whether the busy is even pointed at what matters.

FAQ

What is the most common mistake SME owners make?

Working in the business instead of on it — being the best technician, salesperson or problem-solver rather than building a business that runs without them. It feels like dedication and responsibility, which is exactly why it's so hard to see as a mistake. But an owner trapped in the daily work has no time to build the systems, team and structure that would let the business grow beyond them.

Why is 'working on the business' so hard?

Because working in the business is comfortable, urgent and rewarding — there's always a fire to fight, a customer to serve, a problem only you can solve. Working on the business is none of those: it's not urgent, its rewards are delayed, and it means stepping back from the work you're good at. The urgent crowds out the important. Escaping the trap takes deliberate effort and often an outside push.

How do I delegate without losing control?

By replacing personal oversight with systems and clear results. Delegation fails when it means handing off a task with no structure; it works when there's a clear process, a measurable outcome, and a regular check-in. Control then means seeing the result, not monitoring every step. The fear of losing control is usually really the absence of a system — build the system, and delegation becomes safe.

How do I know if I'm making these mistakes?

Some honest questions: Could the business run for two weeks without you? If not, you're the bottleneck. Do you do things yourself because it's 'faster'? That's the delegation trap. Do you serve every customer and offer every service? That's lost focus. Does a busy day always feel productive? That may be motion mistaken for progress. If these land, they're worth an outside conversation.
