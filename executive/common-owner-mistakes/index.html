The recurring patterns

These mistakes are common

because they're hard to see in yourself.

01

Working in, not on, the business

The owner who's the best worker becomes the bottleneck. Buried in daily operations, there's no time to work on the business — direction, systems, growth. The business can't rise above its owner's task list.

02

Never truly delegating

'It's faster if I do it myself' is true today and fatal long-term. An owner who won't delegate builds a business that can't function, scale, or be sold without them. The refusal to let go is a ceiling.

03

Deciding without numbers

Running on gut feeling works while small. As stakes rise, the owner who can't name their margins, their best customers, or their cash position decides blind — and blindness gets expensive.

04

Saying yes to everything

Every customer, every opportunity, every request. Without a strategy's deliberate 'no', the owner spreads thin across everything and excels at nothing. Focus is a decision most owners never consciously make.

FAQ

What are the most common mistakes SME owners make?

The recurring ones are structural, not stupid: working in the business (daily operations) instead of on it (direction and systems), refusing to truly delegate, deciding without knowing their numbers, and saying yes to everything instead of choosing a focus. These persist because they feel like dedication and hard work from the inside — the owner doing everything looks committed, but is actually the bottleneck.

Why is working 'in' the business a mistake?

Because it makes the owner the ceiling. When the owner is buried in daily operations — the best worker, the fixer of every problem — there's no time or distance to work on the business itself: its direction, systems and growth. The business can't grow beyond what the owner personally handles. Working on the business, not just in it, is what lets it scale beyond one person's daily capacity.

How do I know if I'm making these mistakes?

The honest signs: you can't take a two-week holiday without constant contact (no delegation, no systems), you can't quickly state your margin per customer (deciding without numbers), you're the busiest person and the bottleneck (working in, not on), and you struggle to say no to opportunities (no focus). Most owners recognize at least two — and the recognition is the starting point. An outside perspective makes the blind spots visible.

How do I stop being the bottleneck in my own business?

By deliberately shifting from doing to building: document how key tasks work so others can do them (a master process), delegate genuinely rather than delegating-then-hovering, establish the few numbers that let you steer without controlling every step, and choose a focus so the business does fewer things better. It's a shift from being the best worker to building a business that works without you.
